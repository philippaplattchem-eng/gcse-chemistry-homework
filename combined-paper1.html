Examiner must-knows by sub-topic
T1: Atomic Structure
  • Isotope definition needs both halves: same element (same number of protons) AND different numbers of neutrons. Either half alone scores zero.
  • Atomic number = number of protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons. Neutrons = mass number minus atomic number.
  • For an ion such as X2- with 54 electrons: subtract the charge to find protons. 54 - 2 = 52 protons.
  • Relative atomic mass is a weighted mean and will not always be a whole number. Do not round it.
T1: The Periodic Table
  • Period = horizontal row. Group = vertical column. These are very commonly confused in the exam.
  • Group number = number of outer-shell electrons (for Groups 1–7). This determines reactivity and the ion formed.
  • Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer electrons.
  • Group 1 metals form 1+ ions; Group 2 form 2+; Group 6 non-metals form 2-; Group 7 form 1-.
T2: Ionic Bonding
  • Ionic bonding = electron transfer, not sharing. Draw a dot-and-cross showing electrons moving from metal to non-metal.
  • An ionic lattice has "alternating positive and negative ions in a regular arrangement." Use the word "lattice."
  • Ionic compounds conduct when molten or dissolved because ions are free to move and carry charge. They do not conduct as solids.
  • High melting point + conducts when molten = ionic compound. Do not call this covalent.
T2: Covalent Bonding & Structure
  • Covalent bonding = sharing a pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms. Not transfer.
  • Simple molecular substances (CO2, H2O) have low boiling points because intermolecular forces are weak, not because covalent bonds are weak.
  • Diamond: giant covalent, four bonds per carbon, very high melting point, does not conduct. Graphite: three bonds per carbon, one delocalised electron per atom, conducts electricity.
  • Do not confuse the strength of covalent bonds with the boiling point of simple molecular substances.
T3: Moles & Mass Calculations
  • Moles = mass ÷ Mr. Rearrange the triangle to find mass or Mr as needed.
  • Mr of CaCO3 = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100. The subscript 3 multiplies the whole CO3 group, not just the oxygen.
  • Conservation of mass: if mass decreases in an open container, a gas was produced and escaped. State this explicitly.
  • Show every step of a mole calculation. Method marks are awarded even when the final answer is wrong.
T3: Concentration & Volumes
  • Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000. This is the single most penalised step on Paper 1 across all years.
  • Concentration (mol/dm³) = moles ÷ volume in dm³. Always convert volume before substituting.
  • Write the unit after every calculation answer: mol, g, dm³, mol/dm³, kJ/mol.
  • Check rounding at the end: if the question specifies significant figures or decimal places, match exactly.
T4: Acids, Alkalis & Salts
  • Name salts from the acid used: hydrochloric → chloride; sulfuric → sulfate; nitric → nitrate.
  • Ammonia = NH3. Three hydrogen atoms. Very commonly written incorrectly as NH2 or NH4 in exams.
  • Gas tests: hydrogen gives a squeaky pop with a lit splint; oxygen relights a glowing splint; CO2 turns limewater milky.
  • Sulfate test: acidify with dilute HCl first to remove carbonate interference, then add BaCl2 solution. White precipitate confirms sulfate.
  • Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink/magenta in alkali. Universal indicator: red in strong acid, purple in strong alkali.
T4: Electrolysis
  • Cathode = negative electrode (reduction, ions gain electrons). Anode = positive electrode (oxidation, ions lose electrons). OIL RIG.
  • In aqueous solutions, reactive metals (Na+, K+, Ca2+) are never discharged at the cathode. H+ from water is discharged instead, giving hydrogen gas. Metal only deposits if the metal is below hydrogen in the reactivity series.
  • Brine: chlorine forms at the anode because the solution is concentrated so Cl- outnumbers OH-. In dilute NaCl, oxygen forms instead. Stating this distinction earns the mark.
  • CuSO4 with copper electrodes: anode dissolves at the same rate copper deposits at the cathode, so the blue colour and concentration stay constant. Graphite electrodes: Cu2+ is consumed, the blue colour fades.
T5: Reaction Profiles & Activation Energy
  • Activation energy arrow starts at the reactant energy level and ends at the top of the peak. Not from the product level, not from the bottom of the diagram.
  • A catalyst lowers the activation energy by providing an alternative reaction pathway. It does not change the overall energy change (ΔH).
  • Catalyst exam answers must include "lower activation energy." Writing "speeds up the reaction" alone is not awarded the mark.
  • Exothermic: products lower than reactants on the profile (energy released). Endothermic: products higher than reactants (energy absorbed).
T5: Bond Energies & Energy Changes
  • Bond breaking requires energy input (endothermic). Bond making releases energy (exothermic). Do not reverse these.
  • Overall reaction energy: ΔH = energy in (bonds broken) − energy out (bonds made). Negative ΔH = exothermic.
  • "Energy taken in" describes endothermic, not exothermic. Always check your label matches your explanation.
  • If total energy of bonds made is greater than bonds broken, the reaction is exothermic overall.
